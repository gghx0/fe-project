***

We claim that the complete solution set is exactly the additive involutions of $\mathbb{R}$, which includes the two familiar solutions $f(x)=x$ and $f(x)=-x$, as well as arbitrary non-linear additive involutions.
We now prove that every solution has the claimed form. Let $P(x,y)$ denote the assertion of $f\bigl(f(x)+f(xy)\bigr)=x+xy$.

**Lemma 1.** The function $f$ is surjective.

*Proof.* The assertion $P(x,0)$ yields $f\bigl(f(x)+f(0)\bigr)=x$.
Since every $x\in\mathbb{R}$ occurs on the right-hand side, $f$ must be surjective. $\square$

**Lemma 2.** The function $f$ is injective.

*Proof.* Let $c=f(0)$. From Lemma $1$, we can note that $f\bigl(f(x)+c\bigr)=x$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$. Now suppose that $f(a)=f(b)$. Then
$f(a)+c=f(b)+c$. Applying $f$ to both sides gives $f(f(a)+c)=f(f(b)+c)$, and hence $a=b$. Therefore $f$ is injective. $\square$

**Lemma 3.**
For every $x\neq0$ and every $t\in\mathbb{R}$, $f\bigl(f(x)+f(t)\bigr)=x+t$.

*Proof.* Let $x\neq0$. Given any $t\in\mathbb{R}$, choose $y=\frac{t}{x}$.
Then $xy=t$. Therefore, by $P\left(x,\frac{t}{x}\right)$,

$$
f\bigl(f(x)+f(t)\bigr)
=
x+x\frac{t}{x}
=
x+t.
$$

This proves the lemma. $\square$

**Lemma 4.** For every $x\neq0$ and every $t\in\mathbb{R}$, $f(x)+f(t)=f(x+t)+c$, where $c=f(0)$.

*Proof.* By Lemma $3$, we know that $f\bigl(f(x)+f(t)\bigr)=x+t$. On the other hand, by Lemma $1$ applied to $x+t$, $f\bigl(f(x+t)+c\bigr)=x+t$.
Consequently,

$$
f\bigl(f(x)+f(t)\bigr)
=
f\bigl(f(x+t)+c\bigr).
$$

Since $f$ is injective by Lemma $2$, we have sufficiently proven the lemma. $\square$

**Lemma 5.** The function $g(x)=f(x)-c$ is additive.

*Proof.* Since $c=f(0)$, $g(0)=f(0)-c=0$. If $x\neq0$, Lemma $4$ gives $f(x+t)=f(x)+f(t)-c$. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
g(x+t)
&=f(x+t)-c\\
&=f(x)+f(t)-2c\\
&=(f(x)-c)+(f(t)-c)\\
&=g(x)+g(t).
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $g(x+t)=g(x)+g(t)$ whenever $x\neq0$. If $x=0$, then $g(0+t)=g(t)=g(0)+g(t)$, since $g(0)=0$. Hence for all $x,t\in\mathbb{R}$, $g$ is additive. $\square$

**Lemma 6.** The constant $c=f(0)$ is zero.

*Proof.* By Lemma $1$, $f\bigl(f(x)+c\bigr)=x$. Since $f(x)=g(x)+c$, this becomes $f\bigl(g(x)+2c\bigr)=x$. Since $f=g+c$, we obtain

$$
g\bigl(g(x)+2c\bigr)+c=x.
$$

Because $g$ is additive, $g(g(x))+2g(c)+c=x$. Taking $x=0$ gives $2g(c)+c=0$, where a simple substitution yields $g(g(x))=x\forall x\in\mathbb{R}$. Thus $g$ is an involution. Applying $g$ to $2g(c)+c=0$ gives

$$
2g(g(c))+g(c)=0\implies2c+g(c)=0.
$$

From both of these equations, we get $g(c)=c$. Substituting back yields $3c=0$, meaning since $c\in\mathbb{R}$, $c=0$. Therefore $f=g$. Such means $f$ is additive and $f(f(x))=x$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$. $\square$

**Lemma 7.** Every additive involution is a solution.

*Proof.* Suppose $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is additive and satisfies $f(f(x))=x$
for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$. Then, for arbitrary $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
f\bigl(f(x)+f(xy)\bigr)
&=f(f(x))+f(f(xy))\\
&=x+xy.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $P(x,y)$ holds for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$. $\square$

Therefore the complete solution set is

$$
\boxed{
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
\text{ is additive and }
f\circ f=\operatorname{id}_{\mathbb{R}}.
}
$$

as desired. $\blacksquare$

Note that continuity is not assumed. Consequently, the solution set contains non-linear additive involutions in addition to $f(x)=x$ and $f(x)=-x$.

***

*Remark: Continuity is not assumed.* No continuity hypothesis is needed, and in fact the solution set contains discontinuous functions. To see this explicitly, choose a Hamel basis $B$ of $\mathbb{R}$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ containing $1$ and $\sqrt2$. Define $f$ on $B$ by

$$
f(1)=\sqrt2,\qquad f(\sqrt2)=1,
$$

and

$$
f(b)=b\qquad\text{for every }b\in B\setminus\{1,\sqrt2\}.
$$

Extend $f$ to all of $\mathbb{R}$ by $\mathbb{Q}$-linearity. Then $f$ is additive. Moreover, for every basis element $b\in B$, $f(f(b))=b$. Therefore all of the following are true for every $b\in B\setminus\{1,\sqrt2\}$:

$$
f(f(1))=f(\sqrt2)=1, f(f(\sqrt2))=f(1)=\sqrt2, f(f(b))=b.
$$

By linearity,

$$
f(f(x))=x\forall x\in\mathbb{R}.
$$

Thus $f$ is an additive involution and hence, by the characterization above, is a solution of the original functional equation. It remains to show that $f$ is not continuous. Assume for the sake of contradiction that $f$ were continuous. Every continuous additive function $g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ is linear, so there would exist $c\in\mathbb{R}$ such that $f(x)=cx\forall x\in\mathbb{R}$. Since $f(1)=\sqrt2$, we would have $c=\sqrt{2}$ and consequently $f(\sqrt2)=\sqrt2\cdot\sqrt2=2$. Recall that our construction yielded
$f(\sqrt2)=1$, a contradiction. Hence $f$ is discontinuous. Therefore continuity is not assumed in the problem, and the complete solution set contains discontinuous, non-linear functions. $\square$
