Let $P(x,y)$ denote $f(x(1+f(y)))=(1+y)f(x)$. Taking $x=0$ gives $f(0)=0$. Clearly $f\equiv0$ works, so assume $f\not\equiv0$. If $f(a)=f(b)$, then comparing $P(x,a)$ and $P(x,b)$ for some $x$ with $f(x)\ne0$ gives $(1+a)f(x)=(1+b)f(x)$, hence $a=b$. Thus $f$ is injective. Now $P(x,-1)$ gives $f(x(1+f(-1)))=0=f(0)$, so injectivity gives $f(-1)=-1$. Therefore $P(-1,y)$ gives $f(-1-f(y))=-1-y$. Define $g(t)=1+f(t-1)$. Multiply $x$ by $(1+f(z))$ to get $f(x(1+f(y))(1+f(z)))=(1+y)(1+z)f(x)$. Letting $w=y+z+yz$, we also have $f(x(1+f(w)))=(1+y)(1+z)f(x)$. By injectivity, $1+f(w)=(1+f(y))(1+f(z))$, so $g((1+y)(1+z))=g(1+y)g(1+z)$. Hence $g(ab)=g(a)g(b)$ for all $a,b\in\mathbb R$. Moreover, $f(-1-f(y))=-1-y$ becomes $g(1-g(t))=1-t$. We have $g(0)=0$, $g(1)=1$, and $g(-1)=-1$. If $t>0$, writing $t=u^2$ gives $g(t)=g(u)^2>0$, so $g$ preserves signs. Therefore $g(1-g(t))=1-t$ implies $g(t)>1\iff t>1$. Multiplicativity now shows that $g$ is strictly increasing. Every increasing multiplicative function on $\mathbb R_{>0}$ has the form $g(t)=t^\alpha$ for some $\alpha>0$: indeed, $h(u)=\log g(e^u)$ is increasing and additive, hence $h(u)=\alpha u$. Substituting into $g(1-g(t))=1-t$ gives $(1-t^\alpha)^\alpha=1-t$ for $0<t<1$. Thus $t^{1-\alpha}=\frac{1-(1-t^\alpha)^\alpha}{t^\alpha}\to\alpha$ as $t\to0^+$, which is possible only when $\alpha=1$. Hence $g(t)=t$ for all $t$, so $f(x)=g(x+1)-1=x$. Therefore the solutions are $\boxed{f(x)\equiv0\text{ and }f(x)=x}$.
