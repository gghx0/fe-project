The solutions are

$$
\boxed{f(x)=\lambda x\quad(\lambda\in\mathbb R)},\qquad
\boxed{f(x)=1-x},
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
1,&x=0,\\
0,&x\ne0.
\end{cases}}
$$

Let

$$
P(x,y):\qquad f(x^2+yf(x))=f(x)(x+f(y)).
$$

Putting $x=y=0$ gives $f(0)\in\{0,1\}$.

First suppose $f(0)=0$. Then

$$
P(x,0):\qquad f(x^2)=xf(x),
$$

so $f$ is odd. If $f\equiv0$, we are done. Otherwise choose values with nonzero image. Comparing $P(x,y)$ and $P(-x,y)$ gives, whenever $f(x)\ne0$,

$$
f(x^2+t)+f(x^2-t)=2f(x^2).
$$

Using oddness, this becomes the translation identity

$$
f(t+2x^2)=f(t)+2xf(x). \tag{1}
$$

If $f(t+h)=f(t)+k$ for all $t$, then comparing $P(u,y+h)$ and $P(u,y)$ for any $u$ with $f(u)\ne0$ yields

$$
f(t+hf(u))=f(t)+kf(u). \tag{2}
$$

Also, from $P(x,-x^2/f(x))$,

$$
f\left(\frac{x^2}{f(x)}\right)=x \qquad(f(x)\ne0). \tag{3}
$$

Take $a,b$ with $f(a),f(b)\ne0$. Since $f(b^2)=bf(b)\ne0$, equation (3), applied at $b^2$, gives a $u$ with $f(u)=b^2$. Applying (2) to the translation (1) coming from $a$ gives

$$
f(t+2a^2b^2)=f(t)+2af(a)b^2.
$$

Interchanging $a$ and $b$ gives

$$
af(a)b^2=bf(b)a^2,
$$

so $f(a)/a=f(b)/b$. Hence $f(x)=\lambda x$ whenever $f(x)\ne0$.

There is no nonzero root: if $f(y)=0$ with $y\ne0$, choose $x$ with $f(x)\ne0$. Then $P(x,y)$ gives

$$
f(x^2+yf(x))=xf(x)=f(x^2)\ne0.
$$

Both arguments have nonzero image, so using $f(z)=\lambda z$ on the nonzero-image set forces $y=0$, a contradiction. Thus in this case $f(x)=\lambda x$ for all $x$.

Now suppose $f(0)=1$. From $P(x,0)$,

$$
f(x^2)=(x+1)f(x).
$$

Thus $f(1)=0$, and comparing $x$ with $-x$ gives

$$
(1+x)f(x)=(1-x)f(-x). \tag{4}
$$

Let $x\notin\{-1,0,1\}$ and $f(x)\ne0$, and set $q=(1-x)/(1+x)$. Comparing $P(x,y)$ with $P(-x,qy)$ gives

$$
f(qy)=qf(y)+1-q. \tag{5}
$$

Putting $y=1$ yields $f(q)=1-q$. Since the map $x\mapsto(1-x)/(1+x)$ is an involution, applying the same argument at $q$ gives $f(x)=1-x$ at every nonexceptional point with nonzero image.

If no such point exists, then $f$ vanishes outside $\{-1,0,1\}$, with $f(0)=1$ and $f(1)=0$. Let $c=f(-1)$. From $P(-1,y)$,

$$
f(1+yc)=c(-1+f(y)).
$$

If $c\ne0$, choose $y$ so that both $y$ and $1+yc$ avoid $\{-1,0,1\}$; then the left side is $0$ and the right side is $-c$, impossible. Hence $c=0$, giving the exceptional solution.

Finally, if a nonexceptional $x$ with $f(x)\ne0$ exists, equation (5) produces infinitely many nonexceptional $u$ with $f(u)=1-u$. For arbitrary $y$, choose such a $u$ with $u\ne -f(y)$ and

$$
z=u^2+y(1-u)\notin\{-1,0,1\}.
$$

Then $P(u,y)$ gives

$$
f(z)=(1-u)(u+f(y))\ne0.
$$

So $f(z)=1-z$, and therefore

$$
1-u^2-y(1-u)=(1-u)(u+f(y)).
$$

Since $u\ne1$, this gives $f(y)=1-y$. The listed functions are all directly checked.
