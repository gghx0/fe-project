***

Let $S=\{a\in\mathbb R\mid f(x+a)=f(x)+a\forall x\in\mathbb R\}$, the original statement is equivalent to $xf(x)\in S$ for all $x\in\mathbb R$.
We claim that $S=\{0\}$ or $S=\mathbb R$, let's first consider what happens when $S$ is one of these two sets.

If $S=\{0\}$, we have, since $xf(x)\in S$, $xf(x)=0$ for all $x$, producing solutions of the form $\boxed{f(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x\ne0\\c&\text{if }x=0\end{cases}}$ for a constant $c\in\mathbb R$ which all satisfy the equation.

If $S=\mathbb R$, we have $f(x+y)=f(x)+y$ for all $x,y$, producing solutions of the form $\boxed{f(x)=x+c}$ for a constant $c\in\mathbb R$ which all satisfy the equation.

From now assume $S$ is neither of these two sets.

***

**Claim 1:** $S$ is an additive subgroup of $\mathbb R$, or if $a,b\in S$ then $a+b\in S$ and if $a\in S$ then $-a\in S$

The first is true from $f(x+a+b)=f(x+a)+b=f(x)+a+b$, the second is true from $f(x)=f(x-a)+a$. (also trivially $S$ is nonempty)

***

**Claim 2:** $S$ is a subring (not necessarily with unity) of $\mathbb R$, or if $a,b\in S$ then $ab\in S$

From $xf(x)\in S$ we have, for $a\in S$:
$$
(x+a)f(x+a)\in S\Rightarrow xf(x)+ax+af(x)+a^2\in S,
$$
and, subtracting $xf(x)$, this implies $ax+af(x)+a^2\in S$. So for $a,b\in S$ we have:
$$
ax+af(x)+a^2,bx+bf(x)+b^2\in S\Rightarrow(a+b)x+(a+b)f(x)+a^2+b^2\in S
$$
$$
a+b\in S\Rightarrow(a+b)x+(a+b)f(x)+(a+b)^2\in S
$$
and subtracting these we obtain $2ab\in S$.

This gives $2a^2\in S$, so $2a(x+f(x))=2\left(ax+af(x)+a^2\right)-2a^2\in S$.

Since $S$ is an additive subgroup, any integer multiple of a member is also a member, and in particular, $\frac14f\left(\frac14\right)\in S$ implies $f\left(\frac14\right)\in S$ and $2af\left(\frac14\right)\in S$. Substitute $x=\frac14$ into $2a(x+f(x))$, we get that $a\in S$ implies $\frac a2\in S$ which completes our proof with $\frac{2ab}2\in S$.
***
**Claim 3:** $S$ is a subfield of $\mathbb R$, or if $0\ne a\in S$ then $\frac1a\in S$

With the same technique, we have $0\ne a\in S$ and $xf(x)\in S$, so $axf(x)\in S$ for all $x\in\mathbb R$. Take $x=\frac1a$, and then $f\left(\frac1a\right)\in S$ which we can plug into $2b(x+f(x))\in S$ for $b\in S$ to get $\frac{2b}a\in S$ (since $2bf\left(\frac1a\right)\in S$). Using $a\in S\Rightarrow\frac a2\in S$, this becomes $\frac ba\in S$ for all $0\ne a\in S$, $b\in S$. Take $a=b$ to get $1\in S$, then take $b=1$ to get $\frac1a\in S$ as desired.

***
**Claim 4:** $f\left(x^2\right)=f(x)^2$ for all $x$ such that $x^2\notin S$

From $2a(x+f(x))\in S$ we get $x+f(x)\in S$ (dividing by $2a$ for $0\ne a\in S$), and so can write:
$$
x^2+f\left(x^2\right)\in S,x^2+f(x)^2=\left(x+f(x)\right)^2-2xf(x)\in S
$$
which upon subtraction give $f\left(x^2\right)-f(x)^2\in S$. On the other hand:
$$
x^2f\left(x^2\right)\in S,x^2f(x)^2\in S\Rightarrow x^2f\left(x^2\right)-x^2f(x)^2\in S.
$$
Dividing these two for $f\left(x^2\right)-f(x)^2\ne0$ gives $x^2\in S$ as desired.
***

**Claim 5:** $f(-x)=-f(x)$ for all $x\notin S$

Similar approach:
$$
x+f(x),-x+f(-x)\in S\Rightarrow f(x)+f(-x)\in S
$$
$$
xf(x),-xf(-x)\in S\Rightarrow xf(x)+xf(-x)\in S
$$
so if $f(-x)\ne-f(x)$ then the quotient $x\in S$.
***

**Claim 6:** $f(x)-x$ is bounded

First, we'll show that $\lvert f(x)-x\rvert\le\max\{1,\lvert f(0)\rvert\}$ for all $x\in[0,1)$.

Two cases:

If $x\in S$ then $f\left(0+x\right)=f(0)+x$, i.e. $\lvert f(x)-x\rvert=\lvert f(0)\rvert$.

If $x\notin S$ then $f(x)=f\left(\sqrt x\right)^2\ge0$ and so $f(x)-x>-1$. For the bound above, we have $x-1,1-x\notin S$ so:

$$
f(x)=1+f(x-1)=1-f(1-x)=1-f\left(\sqrt{1-x}\right)^2\le1.
$$
and we get $f(x)\le1$, so $f(x)-x\le1$ as desired.

So we obtain:

$$
\lvert f(x)-x\rvert\le\max\{1,\lvert f(0)\rvert\}
$$
for $x\in[0,1)$, but if it's true for $x$, it's true for $x+1$ and $x-1$ since $f(x+1)=f(x)+1$, and so it holds for all $x$.
***
**Claim 7:** for all $x\in\mathbb R$ and large enough $n\in\mathbb Z$ (based on $x$), $x\notin S$ implies $(x+n)^2\notin S$

Precisely, we prove the statement for all $n>\frac{-x-f(x)}2$.

Suppose that $x\notin S$ yet $(x+n)^2\in S$. As with some of our previous claims:

$$
x+n+f(x+n)\in S
$$
$$
(x+n)^2,(x+n)f(x+n)\in S\Rightarrow(x+n)(x+n+f(x+n))\in S
$$
and since $x+n+f(x+n)=x+f(x)+2n\ne0$ we can divide these to get $x+n\in S$ which implies $x\in S$, contradiction.
***
**Claim 8:** $f(x)=x$ for all $x\notin S$

Take $x\notin S$, we have, for large enough $n\in\mathbb Z$:

$$
f\left(x^2+2nx\right)+n^2=f\left(x^2+2nx+n^2\right)=f\left((x+n)^2\right)=f(x+n)^2=(f(x)+n)^2=f(x)^2+2nf(x)+n^2.
$$
This rearranges as:
$$
\frac{f\left(x^2+2nx\right)-x^2-2nx}{2n}=f(x)-x+\frac{f(x)^2-x^2}{2n}.
$$
Since $f\left(x^2+2nx\right)-x^2-2nx$ is bounded, taking $n\to\infty$ zeroes the $\frac{f\left(x^2+2nx\right)-x^2-2nx}{2n}$ and $\frac{f(x)^2-x^2}{2n}$ terms, implying the claim.

***
With the final claim, we have a contradiction: for $x\notin S$ (which exists since $S\ne\mathbb R$) we have $f(x)=x$. On the other hand, since $x+f(x)\in S$ we derive $2x\in S$ and so $x\in S$. Therefore, our aforementioned solutions are indeed the only ones.
