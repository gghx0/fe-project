We solve

$$
f(f(x)+f(y^2))=f(x)+f(y^2).
$$

Let $\emptyset\ne T\subseteq S\subseteq\mathbb R$ have the closure property that if $a\in S$, $b\in T$, and $a+b\ge0$, then $a+b\in T$. Then all solutions are

$$
\boxed{
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
x,&x\in S+T,\\
g(x),&x\in[0,\infty)\setminus(S+T),\\
h(x),&x\in(-\infty,0)\setminus(S+T),
\end{cases}}
$$

where $S+T=\{a+b\mid a\in S,\ b\in T\}$, $g:[0,\infty)\setminus(S+T)\to T$ is arbitrary, and $h:(-\infty,0)\setminus(S+T)\to S$ is arbitrary.

First, such functions work. For all $x$, we have $f(x)\in S$, and for all $y$, we have $f(y^2)\in T$. Hence

$$
f(x)+f(y^2)\in S+T,
$$

so

$$
f(f(x)+f(y^2))=f(x)+f(y^2).
$$

Conversely, let $f$ be any solution and set

$$
S=f(\mathbb R),\qquad T=f(\mathbb R_{\ge0}).
$$

Then $T\subseteq S$ and both sets are nonempty. If $a\in S$ and $b\in T$, write $a=f(x)$ and $b=f(y^2)$. If $a+b\ge0$, then the equation gives

$$
f(a+b)=a+b,
$$

so $a+b\in T$. Thus the required closure property holds. Moreover, the same identity shows $f(t)=t$ for all $t\in S+T$, while on $[0,\infty)\setminus(S+T)$ the only remaining condition is $f(x)\in T$, and on $(-\infty,0)\setminus(S+T)$ the only remaining condition is $f(x)\in S$. This is exactly the stated family.
