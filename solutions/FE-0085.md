The answer is $f\equiv 0$ and $f\equiv \operatorname{id}$, which both work. Let $P(x,y)$ denote the FE.

$P(-1,-1)$ gives $f(f(1)-1)=f(-1)-f(-1)=0$. Let $c=f(1)-1$, and first suppose that $c\neq 0$. Then $P(x,c/x)$ gives $xf(c/x)=0$, so $f(x)=0$ for $x\neq 0$. From $P(0,0)$, we have $f(f(0))=f(0)$, so $f(0)=0$ in this case and $f\equiv 0$. Hence suppose that $c=0$, so $f(1)=1$ and $f(0)=0$.

$P(1,y)$ gives $f(f(y)+1)=f(y)+1$. This implies $f(2)=2$. Then $P(2,y/2)$ gives that $f(f(y)+2)=2f(y/2)+2$. However $f(f(y)+2)=f(f(f(y)+1)+1)=f(f(y)+1)+1=f(y)+2$, so $f(y)=2f(y/2)$ for all $y$. A corollary is that $f(\frac 12)=\frac 12$.

By $P(f(x),1/f(x))$, we see that $f(f(x)+1)=f(f(x))+f(x)f(1/f(x))$. The LHS is $f(x)+1$,
so $f(f(x))-f(x)=1-f(x)f(1/f(x))$. Plugging in $x/2$ into this equation and simplifying, we get that $\frac 12 \cdot (f(f(x))-f(x))=1-f(x)f(1/f(x))$, so $f(f(x))=f(x)$ for all $x$.

Now we can finish. From $P(x,2)$, we have that $f(x+f(2x))=f(x)+2x$, and from $P(2x,1/2)$, we have $f(2x+f(x))=f(2x)+x$. Hence $x+f(2x)=f(f(x+f(2x)))=f(x+f(2x))=f(x)+2x$, since $f(2x)=2f(x)$ we get that $f(x)=x$, as desired.
