Solution by **AIME4096**.
***

We claim that the complete solution set is characterized as follows. For $x\neq0$, define $q(x)=\frac{f(x)}x$. Then $f$ is a solution if and only if $q:\mathbb{R}^\times\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfies

$$
(q(x)+q(y))q(xy(q(x)+q(y)))=1+q(xy)
$$

whenever $q(x)+q(y)\neq0$, while $q(xy)=-1$ whenever $q(x)+q(y)=0$. Equivalently, every solution is obtained uniquely from such a function $q$ by setting $f(0)=0$ and $f(x)=xq(x)$ for $x\neq0$. Among these solutions are the following explicit families: $f(x)=-\frac{x}{2}$; $f(x)=\chi(x)x$, where $\chi:\mathbb{R}^\times\to\{1,-1\}$ is multiplicative, $\chi(2)=1$, and $\chi(-1)=-1$; and $f(x)=-\frac{1+\chi(x)}2x$, where $\chi:\mathbb{R}^\times\to\{1,-1\}$ is multiplicative, $\chi(2)=1$, and $\chi(-1)=-1$. In particular, $f(x)=x$, $f(x)=|x|$, and $f(x)=-\frac{x}{2}$ are examples of non-pathological solutions, but the solution set is obviously substantially larger than these three functions. Let $P(x,y)$ denote the assertion $f(xf(y)+yf(x))=xy+f(xy)$. We now prove our desired characterization.

**Lemma 1.** $f(0)=0$.

*Proof.* Let $c=f(0)$. Assertion $P(x,0)$ gives $f(xc)=c$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$. Suppose that $c\neq0$. Since multiplication by the nonzero number $c$ is a bijection of $\mathbb{R}$, the numbers $xc$ range over all real numbers. Hence $f(t)=c$ for every $t\in\mathbb{R}$, so $f$ is constant. But if $f\equiv c$, the original equation becomes $c=xy+c$ for every $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$, which is impossible. $\square$

**Lemma 2.** For every $x\neq0$, defining $q(x)=\frac{f(x)}x$ gives the original equation as

$$
f\bigl(xy(q(x)+q(y))\bigr)=xy(1+q(xy)).
$$

*Proof.* For $x,y\neq0$, we have $xf(y)+yf(x)=xyq(y)+xyq(x)=xy(q(x)+q(y))$. Moreover, $xy+f(xy)=xy+xyq(xy)=xy(1+q(xy))$. Substitution into $P(x,y)$ finishes the lemma. Conversely, reversing these calculations recovers $P(x,y)$ whenever $x,y\neq0$. $\square$

**Lemma 3.** Let $x,y\neq0$. If $q(x)+q(y)=0$, then $q(xy)=-1$. If $q(x)+q(y)\neq0$, then

$$
q\bigl(xy(q(x)+q(y))\bigr)=\frac{1+q(xy)}{q(x)+q(y)}.
$$

*Proof.* Suppose first that $q(x)+q(y)=0$. Lemma $2$ gives $f(0)=xy(1+q(xy))$. By Lemma $1$, the left-hand side is $0$. Since $xy\neq0$, we obtain $1+q(xy)=0$, hence $q(xy)=-1$. Now suppose that $q(x)+q(y)\neq0$. Lemma $2$ gives $f\bigl(xy(q(x)+q(y))\bigr)=xy(1+q(xy))$. The argument of $f$ is nonzero, so by definition of $q$,

$$
f\bigl(xy(q(x)+q(y))\bigr)=xy(q(x)+q(y))q\bigl(xy(q(x)+q(y))\bigr).
$$

Dividing by the nonzero number $xy(q(x)+q(y))$ yields

$$
q\bigl(xy(q(x)+q(y))\bigr)=\frac{1+q(xy)}{q(x)+q(y)}.
$$

This proves the lemma. $\square$

**Lemma 4.** Conversely, let $q:\mathbb{R}^\times\to\mathbb{R}$ satisfy the two conditions in Lemma $3$. Define $f(0)=0$ and $f(x)=xq(x)$ for $x\neq0$. Then $f$ satisfies the original functional equation.

*Proof.* Let $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$. If $x=0$ or $y=0$, then $xf(y)+yf(x)=0$ and $xy+f(xy)=0$, so the equation holds. It remains to consider $x,y\neq0$. Put $s=q(x)+q(y)$. If $s=0$, then $xf(y)+yf(x)=xy(q(x)+q(y))=0$, so the left-hand side is $f(0)=0$. By the first condition of Lemma $3$, $q(xy)=-1$, and therefore $xy+f(xy)=xy+xyq(xy)=0$. Thus the equation holds. If $s\neq0$, then Lemma $3$ gives $q(xys)=\frac{1+q(xy)}s$. Consequently,

$$
f(xf(y)+yf(x))=f(xys)=xysq(xys)=xy(1+q(xy))=xy+f(xy).
$$

Thus the original equation holds for every $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$. $\square$

**Lemma 5.** Every solution of the original functional equation is obtained uniquely from a function $q$ satisfying the conditions of Lemma $3$.

*Proof.* Lemma $1$ gives $f(0)=0$. For every nonzero $x$, define $q(x)=\frac{f(x)}{x}$. Lemma $3$ follows directly from the original equation by Lemmas $2$ and $3$. Conversely, Lemma $4$ shows that every such $q$ produces a solution. Note that the correspondence is unique because $q(x)=\frac{f(x)}{x}$ is uniquely determined by $f$ for every $x\neq0$. Therefore the characterization is both necessary and sufficient. $\square$

We next record several concrete families of solutions.

**Lemma 6.** The function $f(x)=-\frac{x}{2}$ is a solution.

*Proof.* For arbitrary $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$, $xf(y)+yf(x)=-\frac{xy}{2}-\frac{xy}{2}=-xy$. Hence $f\bigl(xf(y)+yf(x)\bigr)=f(-xy)=\frac{xy}{2}$, while $xy+f(xy)=xy-\frac{xy}{2}=\frac{xy}{2}$. Thus $P(x,y)$ holds. $\square$

**Lemma 7.** Let $\chi:\mathbb{R}^\times\to\{1,-1\}$ be multiplicative and satisfy $\chi(2)=1$ and $\chi(-1)=-1$. Then $f(0)=0$, $f(x)=\chi(x)\cdot x$ for $x\neq0$ is a solution.

*Proof.* Fix $x,y\neq0$ and write $a=\chi(x)$ and $b=\chi(y)$. If $a=-b$, then $xf(y)+yf(x)=xy(a+b)=0$, so the left-hand side is $0$. Since $\chi(xy)=ab=-1$, $xy+f(xy)=xy(1+\chi(xy))=0$. Suppose now that $a=b$. Then $a+b=2a$, and multiplicativity gives $\chi(xy)=a^2=1$. Therefore

$$
f\bigl(xf(y)+yf(x)\bigr)=f(2axy)=2axy\chi(2axy).
$$

Since $\chi(2)=1$, $\chi(a)=a$, and $\chi(xy)=1$, we have $\chi(2axy)=a$. Hence $f\bigl(xf(y)+yf(x)\bigr)=2a^2xy=2xy$. On the other hand, $xy+f(xy)=xy+xy\chi(xy)=2xy$. The cases involving $x=0$ or $y=0$ follow from $f(0)=0$. Hence $f$ is a solution. $\square$

**Lemma 8.** Let $\chi:\mathbb{R}^\times\to\{1,-1\}$ be multiplicative and satisfy $\chi(2)=1$ and $\chi(-1)=-1$. Then $f(0)=0$,

$$
f(x)=-\frac{1+\chi(x)}{2}\cdot x
$$

for $x\neq0$ is a solution.

*Proof.* Put $q(x)=-\frac{1+\chi(x)}{2}$. Thus $q(x)$ equals $-1$ when $\chi(x)=1$ and $0$ when $\chi(x)=-1$. Let $x,y\neq0$. If $\chi(x)=\chi(y)=-1$, then $q(x)=q(y)=0$, so the left-hand argument is $0$. Since $\chi(xy)=1$, we have $q(xy)=-1$, and therefore the right-hand side is also $0$. If $\chi(x)=\chi(y)=1$, then $q(x)=q(y)=-1$, so the left-hand argument is $-2xy$. Since $\chi(-1)=-1$, $\chi(2)=1$, and $\chi(xy)=1$, we have $\chi(-2xy)=-1$, hence $q(-2xy)=0$. Thus the left-hand side is $0$. Since $q(xy)=-1$, the right-hand side is also $0$. Finally, suppose $\chi(x)\neq\chi(y)$. Then exactly one of $q(x),q(y)$ equals $-1$, so $xf(y)+yf(x)=-xy$. Moreover $\chi(xy)=-1$, so $q(xy)=0$. Since $\chi(-1)=-1$, we have $\chi(-xy)=1$, and hence $q(-xy)=-1$. Therefore $f(-xy)=xy=xy+f(xy)$. Thus the equation holds in every case. $\square$

**Lemma 9.** The characterization above contains the familiar solutions $f(x)=x$ and $f(x)=|x|$.

*Proof.* The identity solution comes from taking $q\equiv1$ in Lemmas $3$ and $4$. Take $\chi(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)$ in Lemma $7$. Since $\operatorname{sgn}(2)=1$ and $\operatorname{sgn}(-1)=-1$, this gives $f(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)x=|x|$. The same sign character in Lemma $8$ gives $f(x)=-x$ for $x>0$, $f(x)=0$ for $x<0$, together with $f(0)=0$. $\square$

Therefore the complete solution set, without any regularity hypothesis, is the class described in Lemmas $3$ and $4$. This characterization is necessary and sufficient, so it gives every solution and no others. In particular, the equation has many non-linear solutions; the three elementary functions $x$, $|x|$, and $-\frac{x}{2}$ are only special cases. $\blacksquare$
