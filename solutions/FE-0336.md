Let $f(0)=c$. For any $t\ge0$, substituting $(x,y)=(0,\sqrt t)$ and $(x,y)=(\sqrt t,0)$ respectively gives

$$
f\bigl(\sqrt t\,f(\sqrt t)\bigr)=f(t),\qquad

f\bigl(\sqrt t\,f(\sqrt t)\bigr)=t+f(0).
$$

Hence $f(t)=t+c$ for all $t\ge0$. Now setting $y=0$ and taking $t>0$ sufficiently large, we get

$$
f\bigl(t f(t)\bigr)=t^2+c.
$$

Since $f(t)=t+c$ and $t(t+c)\ge0$ for sufficiently large $t$, we may use the above formula for nonnegative arguments to obtain $t(t+c)+c=t^2+c$, hence $ct=0$ and therefore $c=0$. Thus

$$
f(t)=t\qquad(t\ge0).
$$

It remains to consider $y<0$. Fix $y<0$ and choose $x>0$ sufficiently large such that $x^2+yf(y)\ge0$. Applying the functional equation and using $f(x)=x$ for $x\ge0$, we obtain

$$
f\bigl(x^2+yf(y)\bigr)=x^2+y^2.
$$

The argument on the left is nonnegative, so $f(u)=u$ gives $x^2+yf(y)=x^2+y^2$, and hence $f(y)=y$. Therefore $f(x)=x$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$.

Finally, $f(x)=x$ indeed satisfies the equation, since

$$
f\bigl(xf(x)+yf(y)\bigr)=f(x^2+y^2)=x^2+y^2=x^2+f(y^2).
$$

Hence the unique solution is $\boxed{f(x)=x}$.
