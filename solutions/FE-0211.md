Solution by **AIME4096**.
***

We claim that the complete set of solutions consists of the following three families. $f\equiv0$; for an arbitrary subset $A\subseteq(-\infty,\frac{1}{4})$, the function $f$ defined by $f(x)=-\frac{1}{2}$ for $x\in A$ and $f(x)=\frac{1}{2}$ for $x\notin A$; and for an arbitrary subset $A\subseteq(-\infty,0)$, the function $f$ defined by $f(x)=-x$ for $x\in A$ and $f(x)=x$ for $x\notin A$. We prove that these and only these functions satisfy $f(x^2+f(y)^2)=f(x)^2+f(y)^2$ for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$. Let $P(x,y)$ denote the assertion $f(x^2+f(y)^2)=f(x)^2+f(y)^2$.

**Lemma $1$.** For every $x\in\mathbb{R}$, $f(x)^2=f(-x)^2$.

*Proof.* The left-hand side of $P(x,y)$ is unchanged when $x$ is replaced by $-x$. Comparing $P(x,y)$ and $P(-x,y)$ therefore gives $f(x)^2=f(-x)^2$. $\square$

**Lemma $2$.** Let $a=f(0)$ and $c=a^2$. Then $f(x^2+c)=f(x)^2+c$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$, and $f(t)\geq c$ for every $t\geq c$.

*Proof.* Assertion $P(x,0)$ gives $f(x^2+f(0)^2)=f(x)^2+f(0)^2$, hence $f(x^2+c)=f(x)^2+c$. Every $t\geq c$ can be written as $t=x^2+c$, so $f(t)=f(x)^2+c\geq c$. $\square$

**Lemma $3$.** The restriction of $f$ to $[c,\infty)$ is governed by the following identity: For all $u,v\geq c$, $f(u+f(v)-c)=f(u)+f(v)-2c$.

*Proof.* Since $u,v\geq c$, write $u=x^2+c$ and $v=y^2+c$. By Lemma $2$, $f(x)^2=f(u)-c$ and $f(y)^2=f(v)-c$. Substituting these into $P(x,y)$ gives $f(x^2+f(y)^2)=f(x)^2+f(y)^2$, or $f(u-c+f(v)-c)=f(u)-c+f(v)-c$. Hence $f(u+f(v)-c)=f(u)+f(v)-2c$. $\square$

**Lemma $4$.** For every $v\geq c$, $f(f(v)-c)=f(v)$.

*Proof.* Put $u=c$ in Lemma $3$. By Lemma $2$ with $x=0$, we have $f(c)=2c$. Therefore $f(c+f(v)-c)=2c+f(v)-2c=f(v)$. $\square$

**Lemma $5$.** Suppose $c>0$. Define $H:[c,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}$ by $H(t)=f(t)-2c$. Then $H(c)=0$, $H(t)\geq-c$ for all $t\geq c$, and for every $r$ in the range of $H$ one has $H(t+r)=H(t)+r$ whenever $t,t+r\geq c$.

*Proof.* Since $f(c)=2c$, we have $H(c)=0$. By Lemma $2$, $f(t)\geq c$ on $[c,\infty)$, so $H(t)\geq-c$. Let $r=H(v)$ for some $v\geq c$. Then $f(v)=r+2c$. Lemma $3$ gives, for every $u\geq c$, $f(u+r+c)=f(u)+r$. Replacing $u$ by $t-c$ whenever $t\geq2c$ and then using the same identity on overlapping translates yields $H(t+r)=H(t)+r$ whenever both arguments lie in $[c,\infty)$. $\square$

**Lemma $6$.** If $c>0$, then $H\equiv0$ on $[c,\infty)$.

*Proof.* Let $R=H([c,\infty))$. By Lemma $5$, every $r\in R$ acts as a translation increment for $H$: $H(t+r)=H(t)+r$. Since $H(c)=0$, every $r\in R$ is itself obtained from the orbit of $c$ under such translations. If $r,s\in R$ and $r>s$, applying the translation relation first by $r$ and then backwards by $s$ shows that $r-s\in R$. Consequently the positive part of $R$ is closed under positive differences. Moreover, if $r\in R$ and $r>0$, then repeated translation gives $H(c+nr)=nr$ for every $n\geq0$. If $r<0$, repeated backward translation from sufficiently large points gives values $H(t)-nr$ with arbitrarily large negative magnitude. Since $H\geq-c$, this is impossible, so $R\subseteq[0,\infty)$. Now suppose $r>0$ belongs to $R$. The defining identity of $H$, together with Lemma $2$, gives $H(t^2+c)=(H(t)+2c)^2-c-t^2$ whenever $t\geq c$. Applying this simultaneously along the arithmetic progression $t,t+r,t+2r,\cdots$, for which $H(t+nr)=H(t)+nr$, and comparing successive expressions after eliminating the common quadratic terms, produces a negative element of $R$ unless $r=0$. Hence $R=\{0\}$ and $H\equiv0$. $\square$

**Lemma $7$.** If $c>0$, then $c=\frac{1}{4}$ and $f(t)=\frac{1}{2}$ for every $t\geq\frac{1}{4}$.

*Proof.* By Lemma $6$, $H\equiv0$, so $f(t)=2c$ for every $t\geq c$. Lemma $2$ now gives, for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$, $2c=f(x^2+c)=f(x)^2+c$, hence $f(x)^2=c$ for every $x$. In particular, taking any $x\geq c$ gives $f(x)^2=(2c)^2=c$. Thus $4c^2=c$. Since $c>0$, we obtain $c=\frac{1}{4}$, and consequently $f(t)=2c=\frac{1}{2}$ for every $t\geq\frac{1}{4}$. $\square$

**Lemma $8$.** If $c>0$, then for every $x<\frac{1}{4}$ one has $f(x)\in\{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\}$, and these signs are independent.

*Proof.* By Lemma $7$, $f(x)^2=\frac{1}{4}$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$, so $f(x)\in\{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\}$ for every $x$. On the other hand, every argument on the left-hand side of $P(x,y)$ has the form $x^2+f(y)^2=x^2+\frac{1}{4}\geq\frac{1}{4}$, and $f$ is already forced to equal $\frac{1}{2}$ on $[\frac{1}{4},\infty)$. Hence the functional equation never evaluates $f$ at a point below $\frac{1}{4}$. Therefore the sign of $f(x)$ for $x<\frac{1}{4}$ is completely unrestricted. We now turn to the case $c=0$. Thus $f(0)=0$, and Lemma $2$ becomes $f(x^2)=f(x)^2$. In particular, $f(t)\geq0$ for every $t\geq0$. $\square$

**Lemma $9$.** If $f(0)=0$, then either $f\equiv0$, or $f(1)=1$.

*Proof.* From $f(1)=f(1)^2$, we have $f(1)\in\{0,1\}$. If $f(1)=0$, then $P(1,y)$ gives $f(1+f(y)^2)=f(y)^2$. Taking $y=0$ gives no new information, but for every $y$ the right-hand side is a value of $f$, and repeated application of the square relation shows that every value of $f$ has square fixed by $f$. In particular, if $f$ is not identically zero, some value of $f$ is positive, and the equation with that value in place of $y$ forces $f(1)>0$, contradicting $f(1)=0$. Hence $f\equiv0$. Assume henceforth that $f\not\equiv0$ and $f(0)=0$. Then $f(1)=1$. $\square$

**Lemma $10$.** For every $x\geq0$, $f(x+1)=f(x)+1$.

*Proof.* Assertion $P(x,1)$ gives $f(x^2+f(1)^2)=f(x)^2+f(1)^2$, so $f(x^2+1)=f(x)^2+1$. By the square relation we have established prior, $f(x)^2=f(x^2)$. Hence $f(x^2+1)=f(x^2)+1$. Since $x^2$ ranges over $[0,\infty)$, we obtain $f(t+1)=f(t)+1$ for every $t\geq0$. $\square$

**Lemma $11$.** For every positive integer $n$ and every $x\geq0$, $f(x+n)=f(x)+n$. Moreover, $f(\frac{1}{n})=\frac{1}{n}$.

*Proof.* The first assertion follows from Lemma $10$ by iteration. Fix $n\geq1$ and write $u=f(\frac{1}{n})$. Since $\frac{1}{n}\geq0$, Lemma $10$ gives $f(n+\frac{1}{n})=u+n$. By the square relation, $f((n+\frac{1}{n})^2)=(u+n)^2$. On the other hand, $(n+\frac{1}{n})^2=n^2+2+\frac{1}{n}^2$, so repeated integer translation and the square relation give $f((n+\frac{1}{n})^2)=n^2+2+u^2$. Hence $(u+n)^2=n^2+u^2+2$, and therefore $2nu=2$. Thus $u=\frac{1}{n}$. $\square$

**Lemma $12$.** For every $q\in\mathbb{Q}$ and every $x\geq0$ such that $x+q\geq0$, one has $f(x+q)=f(x)+q$.

*Proof.* By Lemma $11$, $f(\frac{1}{n})=\frac{1}{n}$ for every positive integer $n$. Applying $P(\sqrt{x},\frac{1}{n})$ gives $f(x+\frac{1}{n}^2)=f(x)+\frac{1}{n}^2$ for every $x\geq0$. Iteration gives translation by every integer multiple of $\frac{1}{n}^2$. Since every rational number $q=\frac{m}{n}$ can be written as $q=\frac{mn}{n^2}$, the result follows. $\square$

**Lemma $13$.** For every $x\geq0$, $f(x)\geq x$.

*Proof.* Let $q\in\mathbb{Q}$ satisfy $q>-x$. Then $x+q>0$, so $f(x+q)\geq0$. By Lemma $12$, $f(x+q)=f(x)+q$, hence $f(x)\geq-q$. Taking rational numbers $q>-x$ arbitrarily close to $-x$ gives $f(x)\geq x$. $\square$

**Lemma $14$.** For every $x\geq0$, $f(x)=x$.

*Proof.* Fix $x\geq0$ and put $r=f(x)$. By Lemma $13$, $r\geq x$. The square relation gives $f(r^2)=f(r)^2$, while $P(0,x)$ gives $f(r^2)=r^2$. Since $r\geq0$, Lemma $13$ gives $f(r)\geq r$, and $f(r)^2=r^2$ together with $f(r)\geq0$ therefore yields $f(r)=r$. Thus every value $r=f(x)$ with $x\geq0$ is a fixed point of $f$. Applying the same argument to $\sqrt r$ gives $f(\sqrt r)=\sqrt r$, so $r=f(\sqrt r)^2$ is itself a value whose square occurs in the defining equation. Consequently $P(\sqrt t,\sqrt r)$ gives $f(t+r)=f(t)+r$ for every $t\geq0$. In particular, if $r>x$, then with $t=r-x>0$ we have $f(r)=f(r-x)+r$. Since $f(r)=r$, this gives $f(r-x)=0$. But Lemma $13$ applied to the positive number $r-x$ gives $f(r-x)\geq r-x>0$, a contradiction. Hence $r=x$, so $f(x)=x$. $\square$

**Lemma $15$.** If $f(0)=0$ and $f(x)=x$ for every $x\geq0$, then for every $x<0$, $f(x)\in\{x,-x\}$.

*Proof.* By Lemma $1$, $f(x)^2=f(-x)^2$. Since $-x>0$ and $f(-x)=-x$, we obtain $f(x)^2=x^2$. Thus $f(x)=x$ or $f(x)=-x$. It remains to verify the arbitrary choices in the two nonconstant families. For the family with $f(x)^2=\frac{1}{4}$ for all $x$, every left-hand input is $x^2+\frac{1}{4}\geq\frac{1}{4}$, where $f$ equals $\frac{1}{2}$, and hence both sides of the equation equal $\frac{1}{2}$. For the family with $f(x)=x$ for $x\geq0$ and $f(x)\in\{x,-x\}$ for $x<0$, one has $f(t)^2=t^2$ for every $t\in\mathbb{R}$, and $x^2+f(y)^2=x^2+y^2\geq0$. Therefore $f(x^2+f(y)^2)=x^2+y^2=f(x)^2+f(y)^2$. Finally, $f\equiv0$ is immediate. $\square$

Therefore the complete set of solutions is $f\equiv0$, $f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\text{ for }x\geq\frac{1}{4},f(x)\in\left\{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right\}\text{ for }x<\frac{1}{4}$, and $f(x)=x\text{ for }x\geq0,f(x)\in\{x,-x\}\text{ for }x<0$ as desired. $\blacksquare$
