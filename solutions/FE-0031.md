Let $P(x,y)$ denote

$$
f(x+f(y)^2)=f(x)+f(y^2)
$$

for all real $x$ and $y$.

Put $a=f(0)$. From $P(x,0)$,

$$
f(x+a^2)=f(x)+a. \tag{1}
$$

Taking $x=-a^2$ gives $f(-a^2)=0$. Hence $P(0,-a^2)$ gives $f(a^4)=0$. Also, $P(0,0)$ gives $f(a^2)=2a$, so $P(x,a^2)$ gives

$$
f(x+4a^2)=f(x).
$$

But applying (1) four times gives

$$
f(x+4a^2)=f(x)+4a.
$$

Therefore $a=0$. Thus

$$
f(0)=0,
$$

and

$$
f(f(y)^2)=f(y^2). \tag{2}
$$

Suppose first that $f(y^2)=0$ for every $y$. Then $f$ is zero on all nonnegative real numbers, and the original equation becomes

$$
f(x+f(y)^2)=f(x).
$$

If $f(y)\ne0$ for some $y$, let

$$
q=f(y)^2>0.
$$

Then

$$
f(y+nq)=f(y)
$$

for every positive integer $n$. For sufficiently large $n$, $y+nq\ge0$, so $f(y+nq)=0$, a contradiction. Hence $f$ is identically zero.

Now suppose $f$ is not identically zero. Then there is some $u$ such that

$$
c=f(u^2)\ne0.
$$

Set

$$
p=f(u)^2.
$$

By (2), $f(p)=c$, so $p>0$. From $P(x,u)$,

$$
f(x+p)=f(x)+c. \tag{3}
$$

We claim that $f$ has no nonzero period. Suppose $h\ne0$ and

$$
f(x+h)=f(x)
$$

for every $x$. Set

$$
t=\frac{p-h^2}{2h}.
$$

Then

$$
(t+h)^2=t^2+p.
$$

Since $f(t+h)=f(t)$, equation (2) gives

$$
f((t+h)^2)=f(f(t+h)^2)=f(f(t)^2)=f(t^2).
$$

However, (3) gives

$$
f((t+h)^2)=f(t^2+p)=f(t^2)+c,
$$

contradicting $c\ne0$. Thus $f$ has no nonzero period.

Comparing $P(x,y)$ and $P(x,-y)$, we see that

$$
f(y)^2-f(-y)^2
$$

is a period. Therefore

$$
f(-y)^2=f(y)^2. \tag{4}
$$

By (3),

$$
f(y+p)=f(y)+c,
$$

and

$$
f(-y-p)=f(-y)-c.
$$

Applying (4) to $y+p$ gives

$$
(f(-y)-c)^2=(f(y)+c)^2.
$$

Together with $f(-y)^2=f(y)^2$ and $c\ne0$, this gives

$$
f(-y)=-f(y). \tag{5}
$$

For brevity, write $h\to r$ if

$$
f(x+h)=f(x)+r
$$

for every $x$. Direct substitution shows that $h\to r$ and $k\to s$ imply

$$
h+k\to r+s
$$

and

$$
h-k\to r-s.
$$

Moreover, if $h\to r$ and $k\to r$, then $h-k$ is a period. Since there is no nonzero period, $h=k$.

The original equation says

$$
f(y)^2\to f(y^2). \tag{6}
$$

In particular, $p\to c$. Let

$$
d=c^2.
$$

Since $f(p)=c$, equation (6) shows that $d$ is also an allowable shift.

Let $h\to r$, and set

$$
z=\frac{h-p^2}{2p}.
$$

Then

$$
(z+p)^2=z^2+h.
$$

By (6), the shift

$$
f(z+p)^2-f(z)^2
$$

has increment

$$
f((z+p)^2)-f(z^2)=r.
$$

It therefore has the same increment as $h$, so the absence of nonzero periods gives

$$
h=f(z+p)^2-f(z)^2
$$

$$
=(f(z)+c)^2-f(z)^2
$$

$$
=2cf(z)+d. \tag{7}
$$

Consequently,

$$
\frac{(h-d)^2}{4d}=f(z)^2
$$

is an allowable shift.

If $v$ is an allowable shift, then $d+v$ is also one. Applying the preceding result to $d+v$ shows that

$$
\frac{v^2}{4d}
$$

is an allowable shift. Hence

$$
\frac v2
=
\frac{(d+v)^2}{4d}
-
\frac{d^2}{4d}
-
\frac{v^2}{4d}
$$

is also an allowable shift. Starting with $d$, we conclude that every number

$$
\frac{md}{2^n},
$$

where $m$ is an integer and $n$ is a nonnegative integer, is an allowable shift. These numbers are dense in the real line.

Now let $h\to r$ and suppose $x^2+h\ge0$. Choose $z$ such that

$$
z^2=x^2+h.
$$

By (6), the shift

$$
f(z)^2-f(x)^2
$$

has increment

$$
f(z^2)-f(x^2)=f(x^2+h)-f(x^2)=r.
$$

It has the same increment as $h$, so

$$
f(z)^2=f(x)^2+h. \tag{8}
$$

If $f(x)^2<x^2$ for some $x$, density allows us to choose an allowable shift $h$ satisfying

$$
-x^2<h<-f(x)^2.
$$

Then $x^2+h>0$, while (8) gives

$$
f(z)^2<0,
$$

which is impossible. Therefore

$$
f(x)^2\ge x^2 \tag{9}
$$

for every real $x$.

Take $h=d$ in (7). For

$$
z=\frac{d-p^2}{2p},
$$

equation (7) gives $f(z)=0$. By (9), $z=0$, so $d=p^2$. Since $d=c^2$ and $p>0$,

$$
c=\varepsilon p
$$

for some $\varepsilon\in{1,-1}$. Thus (3) becomes

$$
f(x+p)=f(x)+\varepsilon p. \tag{10}
$$

Define

$$
g(x)=f(x)-\varepsilon x.
$$

Then

$$
g(x+p)=g(x),
$$

so

$$
g(x+np)=g(x)
$$

for every integer $n$. By (9),

$$
0\le f(x+np)^2-(x+np)^2
$$

$$
=2\varepsilon g(x)(x+np)+g(x)^2.
$$

If $g(x)\ne0$, the right-hand side is negative for sufficiently large positive or negative $n$, a contradiction. Hence $g(x)=0$ for every $x$, and therefore

$$
f(x)=\varepsilon x.
$$

Direct substitution shows that both signs work. Hence all solutions are

$$
\boxed{f(x)=0,\quad f(x)=x,\quad f(x)=-x}.
$$
