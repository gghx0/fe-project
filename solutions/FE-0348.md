The solutions are exactly the functions obtained as follows. Choose a set $C\subseteq\mathbb R$ such that

$$
0\notin C,\qquad x\in C\implies x^2\in C,
$$

and

$$
x,y\in C\implies x^2+y^2\in C.
$$

Then define

$$
\boxed{
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
x,&x\in C,\\
0,&x\notin C.
\end{cases}}
$$

We prove that these are the only solutions. From $P(0,x)$,

$$
f(f(x)^2)=xf(x).
$$

Putting $x=0$ gives $f(f(0)^2)=0$, and then putting $x=f(0)^2$ gives $f(0)=0$.

If $f(a)=f(b)\ne0$, then the equation above gives $af(a)=bf(a)$, so $a=b$. Thus $f$ is injective on the set where it is nonzero.

Let

$$
C=\{x:f(x)\ne0\}.
$$

For $x\in C$, comparing

$$
P(x,0):\quad f(xf(x))=xf(x)
$$

with

$$
P(0,x):\quad f(f(x)^2)=xf(x)
$$

and using injectivity on nonzero values gives $xf(x)=f(x)^2$, hence $f(x)=x$. Therefore $f$ has the displayed form.

Moreover, if $x\in C$, then $P(x,0)$ gives $f(x^2)=x^2$, so $x^2\in C$. If $x,y\in C$, then $P(x,y)$ gives

$$
f(x^2+y^2)=x^2+y^2,
$$

so $x^2+y^2\in C$.

Conversely, any function built from such a set $C$ works by a direct case check according as $x$ and $y$ lie in $C$.
