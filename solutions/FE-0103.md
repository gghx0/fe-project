**Note: we show the solution for the statement of BMO 1997/4, which has $x$ and $y$ swapped.**
***
Solution by **prester**.
***

We claim the only solutions are $f(x)\equiv x$ and $f(x)\equiv -x$, which obviously work. We now show they are the only solutions. Let $P(x,y)$ denote the assertion that $f(xf(x)+f(y))=f(x)^2+y$. 
***

$ P(0,y)$ $ \implies f(f(y))=f^2(0)+y\qquad(*)$

Therefore $ f(x)$ is a bijection of $ \mathbb {R}$.

So $ \exists u\in\mathbb{R}$ such that $ f(u)=0$

$ P(u,y)$ $ \implies f(f(y))=y\qquad(**)$

So $ f(0)=0$ and $ u=0$.

$ P(f(x),y)$ $ \implies f(xf(x) + f(y))=x^2+y\qquad (***)$

Combining $P(x,y)$ and $ (***)$ we have $ f^2(x)=x^2 \qquad \forall x\in\mathbb R$.

Therefore for $ x_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ we have either $ f(x_0)=x_0$ or $ f(x_0)=-x_0$

Let $ x_0,y_0$ be non-zero reals with $ f(x_0)=x_0$ and $ f(y_0)=-y_0$.

$ P(x_0,-y_0)$ $ \implies f(x_0^2+y_0)=x_0^2-y_0$ so $x_0y_0=0$, a contradiction.

So we may conclude that $ \forall x\in \mathbb{R}$ we have either $ f(x)=x$ or $ f(x)=-x$
