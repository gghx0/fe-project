Plugging $y=0$ gives

$$
f(0)=0.
$$

If $f(k)=0$ for some nonzero $k$, then plugging $x=k$ gives

$$
f(ky)=0
$$

so $f$ is constantly $0$, which is a solution.

Otherwise we have $f(x)=0$ if and only if $x=0$. If $f(a)=f(b)$ for distinct $a,b$, then both $a,b$ are nonzero so $f(a)$ is nonzero. Plug $x=a$ and

$$
y=\frac{b-a}{f(a)}
$$

gives

$$
f(b)=f(a)+f(ay)
$$

so $f(ay)=0$, which is a contradiction as $ay$ is nonzero. Hence $f$ is injective.

Plugging $x=1$ gives

$$
f(1+yf(1))=f(1)+f(y).
$$

For nonzero $x$, plugging $y=1/x$ gives

$$
f\left(x+\frac{f(x)}{x}\right)=f(x)+f(1).
$$

This gives

$$
f(1+xf(1))=f\left(x+\frac{f(x)}{x}\right)
$$

so by injectivity we get

$$
f(x)=cx^2+x,
$$

where

$$
c=f(1)-1.
$$

This is also true for $x=0$. Therefore

$$
f(x)=cx^2+x
$$

for all $x$. If $c$ is nonzero then $f$ is a quadratic so can't be injective, hence $c=0$, so

$$
f(x)=x,
$$

which works.

Therefore the solutions are

$$
\boxed{f(x)=0\quad\text{and}\quad f(x)=x}.
$$
