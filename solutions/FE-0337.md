The solutions are as follows. Let $\{0\}\subseteq S\subseteq\mathbb R$ be a set satisfying the closure property

$$
a,b\in S\implies (a+b)^2\in S.
$$

Then

$$
\boxed{
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
x,& x\in S+S,\\
\dfrac{g(x)}{x},& x\notin S+S,
\end{cases}}
$$

where

$$
S+S=\{a+b\mid a,b\in S\}
$$

and $g:\mathbb R\setminus(S+S)\to S$ is arbitrary.

First we show that all such functions work. If $x\in S+S$, then $x=a+b$ for some $a,b\in S$, so by the closure property

$$
xf(x)=x^2=(a+b)^2\in S.
$$

If $x\notin S+S$, then $x\ne0$, since $0\in S+S$, and

$$
xf(x)=g(x)\in S.
$$

Therefore $xf(x)\in S$ for all $x$. Hence $xf(x)+yf(y)\in S+S$, so

$$
f(xf(x)+yf(y))=xf(x)+yf(y),
$$

as required.

Conversely, suppose $f$ is a solution and define

$$
S=\{xf(x)\mid x\in\mathbb R\}.
$$

Clearly $0\in S$. The equation gives $f(a+b)=a+b$ for all $a,b\in S$, so

$$
(a+b)^2=(a+b)f(a+b)\in S.
$$

Thus $S$ has the required closure property. Also, for every $x\in S+S$, the equation gives $f(x)=x$. For $x\notin S+S$, we have $x\ne0$ and $xf(x)\in S$, so writing $g(x)=xf(x)$ gives

$$
f(x)=\frac{g(x)}{x}.
$$

This is exactly the claimed form, completing the classification.
