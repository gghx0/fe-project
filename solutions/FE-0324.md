We claim that the complete solution set is $f(x)\equiv0,f(x)=x\forall x\in\mathbb{R}$. Both functions clearly satisfy $f\bigl(x^2+f(xy)\bigr)=f(x)^2+yf(x)$ for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$. We now prove that these are the only solutions. Let $P(x,y)$ denote the assertion $f\bigl(x^2+f(xy)\bigr)=f(x)^2+yf(x)$.

**Lemma 1.** $f(0)=0$.

*Proof.* Assertion $P(0,y)$ gives $f(f(0))=f(0)^2+yf(0)$ for every $y\in\mathbb{R}$. Taking $y=0$ and $y=1$ gives $f(f(0))=f(0)^2$ and $f(f(0))=f(0)^2+f(0)$, respectively. Hence $f(0)=0$. $\square$

**Lemma 2.** For every $a\neq0$, define the affine map $T_a:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2$ by $T_a(u,v)=\left(a^2+v,f(a)^2+\frac{f(a)}a u\right)$. If $\Gamma={(z,f(z)):z\in\mathbb{R}}$ is the graph of $f$, then $T_a(\Gamma)\subseteq\Gamma$.

*Proof.* Let $(z,f(z))\in\Gamma$. Since $a\neq0$, put $y=\frac{z}{a}$ in $P(a,y)$. Then $ay=z$, so
$$
f\left(a^2+f(z)\right)=f(a)^2+\frac{f(a)}a z.
$$
Hence $T_a(z,f(z))\in\Gamma$. Therefore $T_a(\Gamma)\subseteq\Gamma$. $\square$

**Lemma 3.** If $f\not\equiv0$, then there exists $a\neq0$ such that $f(a)\neq0$.

*Proof.* Since $f\not\equiv0$, there exists $a\in\mathbb{R}$ with $f(a)\neq0$. By Lemma $1$, $f(0)=0$, so necessarily $a\neq0$. $\square$

**Lemma 4.** If $f\not\equiv0$, then $f$ is injective.

*Proof.* Choose $a\neq0$ with $f(a)\neq0$ by Lemma $3$. Suppose $f(u)=f(v)$. Applying $T_a$ to $(u,f(u))$ and $(v,f(v))$ gives two points of $\Gamma$ whose first coordinates are equal, namely $a^2+f(u)=a^2+f(v)$. Since $\Gamma$ is the graph of a function, those two points must have the same second coordinate. Hence
$$
f(a)^2+\frac{f(a)}a\cdot u=f(a)^2+\frac{f(a)}a\cdot v.
$$
Since $a\neq0$ and $f(a)\neq0$, we obtain $u=v$. Thus $f$ is injective. $\square$

**Lemma 5.** If $f\not\equiv0$, then $f$ is surjective.

*Proof.* Choose $a\neq0$ with $f(a)\neq0$. By Lemma $2$, for every $z\in\mathbb{R}$, the point $T_a(z,f(z))=\left(a^2+f(z),f(a)^2+\frac{f(a)}a z\right)$ belongs to $\Gamma$. Let $r\in\mathbb{R}$ be arbitrary. Since $\frac{f(a)}a\neq0$, choose $z=\frac{a}{f(a)}\bigl(r-f(a)^2\bigr)$. Then the second coordinate of $T_a(z,f(z))$ is $r$. Since the resulting point lies on $\Gamma$, its first coordinate, say $u$, satisfies $f(u)=r$. Thus every $r\in\mathbb{R}$ is attained by $f$, so $f$ is surjective. $\square$

**Lemma 6.** If $f\not\equiv0$, then $f(-x)=-f(x)$ for every $x\neq0$.

*Proof.* Fix $x\neq0$. Putting $y=\frac{t}{x}$ in $P(x,y)$ gives $f\bigl(x^2+f(t)\bigr)=f(x)^2+\frac{t}{x}f(x)$. Putting $y=-\frac{t}{x}$ in $P(-x,y)$ gives $f\bigl(x^2+f(t)\bigr)=f(-x)^2-\frac{t}{x}f(-x)$. Therefore
$$
f(x)^2-f(-x)^2+\frac{t}{x}\bigl(f(x)+f(-x)\bigr)=0
$$
for every $t\in\mathbb{R}$. Taking $t=0$ gives $f(x)^2=f(-x)^2$, and then taking $t=x$ gives $f(x)+f(-x)=0$. Hence $f(-x)=-f(x)$. $\square$

**Lemma 7.** If $f\not\equiv0$, then $0$ is the unique zero of $f$.

*Proof.* By Lemma $5$, there exists $r\in\mathbb{R}$ such that $f(r)=0$. Assume for the sake of contradiction $r\neq0$. Then Lemma $6$ gives $f(-r)=0$. Since $f$ is injective by Lemma $4$, we obtain $r=-r$, hence $r=0$, a contradiction. Therefore every zero of $f$ is $0$. $\square$

**Lemma 8.** If $f\not\equiv0$, then $f(x)>0$ whenever $x>0$, and $f(x)<0$ whenever $x<0$.

*Proof.* Setting assertion $P(x,0)$ and using Lemma $1$ gives $f(x^2)=f(x)^2$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$. If $t>0$, write $t=x^2$ with $x\neq0$. Then $f(t)=f(x)^2$. By Lemma $7$, $f(x)\neq0$, so $f(t)>0$. If $t<0$, then $-t>0$, so $f(-t)>0$, and Lemma $6$ gives $f(t)=-f(-t)<0$. $\square$

**Lemma 9.** If $f\not\equiv0$, then $T_a(\Gamma)=\Gamma$ for every $a\neq0$.

*Proof.* By Lemma $7$, $f(a)\neq0$ for every $a\neq0$, so $T_a$ is an invertible affine map. Lemma $2$ gives $T_a(\Gamma)\subseteq\Gamma$. The first coordinate of $T_a(z,f(z))$ is $a^2+f(z)$. Since $f$ is surjective by Lemma $5$, this first coordinate assumes every real value. Thus $T_a(\Gamma)$ contains a point above every real first coordinate. Since $\Gamma$ contains exactly one point above each first coordinate, we obtain $T_a(\Gamma)=\Gamma$ as desired. $\square$

**Lemma 10.** For every $a\neq0$, put $c_a=\frac{f(a)}a$. Then $c_a>0$. Moreover, if $S_a=T_a^2$, then, identifying points of $\mathbb{R}^2$ with vectors $X$, we have $S_a(X)=c_aX+W_a$, where $W_a=\left(a^2+f(a)^2,f(a)^2+c_a a^2\right)$.

*Proof.* By Lemma $8$, $a$ and $f(a)$ have the same sign, so $c_a>0$. A direct computation gives $S_a(u,v)=\left(a^2+f(a)^2+c_a u,f(a)^2+c_a a^2+c_a v\right)$, which is exactly $S_a(X)=c_aX+W_a$. Since $T_a(\Gamma)=\Gamma$ by Lemma $9$, we also have $S_a(\Gamma)=\Gamma$. $\square$

**Lemma 11.** Suppose that $f\not\equiv0$ and $f\neq\operatorname{id}$. Then there exist $a,b\neq0$ such that $c_a\neq1$ and $c_b\neq c_a$.

*Proof.* If $c_x=1$ for every $x\neq0$, then $f(x)=x$ for every $x\neq0$, and Lemma $1$ gives $f(0)=0$, so $f=\operatorname{id}$, contrary to hypothesis. Hence there exists $a\neq0$ with $c_a\neq1$. Suppose that $c_x=c_a=:c$ for every $x\neq0$. Then $f(x)=cx$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$. Assertion $P(x,0)$ in the original equation gives $f(x^2)=f(x)^2$, hence $cx^2=c^2x^2$ for every $x$. Taking $x=1$ gives $c=c^2$. Since $c>0$ by Lemma $10$, we obtain $c=1$, contradicting $c_a\neq1$. Therefore some $b\neq0$ satisfies $c_b\neq c_a$. $\square$

**Lemma 12.** Suppose that $f\not\equiv0$ and $f\neq\operatorname{id}$. Then $\Gamma$ possesses a nonzero translation symmetry.

*Proof.* Let $a,b$ be given by Lemma $11$, and write $c=c_a$ and $d=c_b$. If $d=1$, then Lemma $10$ gives $S_b(X)=X+W_b$. Since $f(b)=b$, we have $W_b=(2b^2,2b^2)\neq(0,0)$. Thus $S_b$ is a nonzero translation preserving $\Gamma$. Assume now that $d\neq1$. Since $c\neq1$ as well, both $S_a$ and $S_b$ are homotheties. Let $C_a$ and $C_b$ be their respective fixed points. Since $S_r(X)=c_rX+W_r$ and $c_r\neq1$, its unique fixed point is $C_r=\frac{1}{1-c_r}W_r$. Since $f(r)=c_r r$, this becomes
$$
C_r=\frac{r^2}{1-c_r}\left(1+c_r^2,c_r(c_r+1)\right).
$$
We now claim that $C_a\neq C_b$. Suppose otherwise for the sake of contradiction. Since $a,b\neq0$ and $c,d\neq1$, the first coordinates of $C_a$ and $C_b$ are nonzero. Hence equality of the ratios of their second and first coordinates gives $\frac{c(c+1)}{1+c^2}=\frac{d(d+1)}{1+d^2}$. Cross-multiplication yields $(c-d)(cd-c-d-1)=0$. Since $c\neq d$, we obtain $cd-c-d-1=0$, or equivalently $d=\frac{c+1}{c-1}$. Because $c>0$ and $d>0$, this equality forces $c>1$ and $d>1$. Substituting $d=\frac{c+1}{c-1}$ gives $\frac{1+d^2}{1-d}=\frac{1+c^2}{1-c}$. Equality of the first coordinates of $C_a$ and $C_b$ therefore gives $a^2=b^2$. Hence $b=a$ or $b=-a$. By Lemma $6$, in either case $f(b)=\pm f(a)$ with the same sign as $b=\pm a$, and consequently $\frac{f(b)}b=\frac{f(a)}a$. Thus $c_b=c_a$, contradicting the choice of $a$ and $b$. Therefore $C_a\neq C_b$.

Now write $A=S_a$ and $B=S_b$. Since $A$ and $B$ are homotheties centered at $C_a$ and $C_b$, respectively, we have $A(X)=cX+(1-c)C_a$ and $B(X)=dX+(1-d)C_b$. Their inverses are
$$
A^{-1}(X)=\frac1cX+\left(1-\frac1c\right)C_a\text{ and }B^{-1}(X)=\frac1dX+\left(1-\frac1d\right)C_b.
$$
Consider the commutator $K=ABA^{-1}B^{-1}$. Since the linear parts of $A$, $B$, $A^{-1}$, and $B^{-1}$ are $c$, $d$, $\frac{1}{c}$, and $\frac{1}{d}$, respectively, the linear part of $K$ is $1$. Hence $K$ is a translation. We compute the translation vector directly. Conjugating $B$ by $A$ gives $ABA^{-1}(X)=dX+(1-d)A(C_b)$. Therefore $K(X)=ABA^{-1}\bigl(B^{-1}(X)\bigr)=X+(d-1)C_b+(1-d)A(C_b)$. Since $A(C_b)=cC_b+(1-c)C_a$, we obtain $K(X)=X+(d-1)C_b+(1-d)cC_b+(1-d)(1-c)C_a$, and collecting terms gives $K(X)=X+(c-1)(d-1)(C_a-C_b)$. Thus $K$ is the translation by the vector $(c-1)(d-1)(C_a-C_b)$. This vector is nonzero because $c\neq1$, $d\neq1$, and $C_a\neq C_b$. By Lemma $9$, $A(\Gamma)=\Gamma$ and $B(\Gamma)=\Gamma$. Hence $A^{-1}(\Gamma)=\Gamma$ and $B^{-1}(\Gamma)=\Gamma$, so $K(\Gamma)=\Gamma$. Therefore $\Gamma$ possesses a nonzero translation symmetry. $\square$

**Lemma 13.** Any nonzero translation symmetry of $\Gamma$ has a nonzero horizontal component.

*Proof.* Suppose translation by $(0,k)$ preserves $\Gamma$. Then for every $(x,f(x))\in\Gamma$, the point $(x,f(x)+k)$ also belongs to $\Gamma$. Since $\Gamma$ is the graph of a function, there is exactly one point of $\Gamma$ with first coordinate $x$. Hence $k=0$. Therefore every nonzero translation symmetry has a nonzero horizontal component. $\square$

**Lemma 14.** Suppose that $f\not\equiv0$ and $f\neq\operatorname{id}$. Then there exists a dense additive subgroup $H\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ and a constant $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}$ such that $f(x+t)=f(x)+\lambda t$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$ and every $t\in H$.

*Proof.* By Lemma $12$, choose a nonzero translation symmetry $(h,k)$ of $\Gamma$. By Lemma $13$, $h\neq0$. Let $c=c_a$, where $a$ is the element chosen in Lemma $11$. Since $c>0$ and $c\neq1$, $S_a$ is a homothety of ratio $c$. Conjugating the translation by $(h,k)$ with $S_a^r$ gives a translation by $(c^rh,c^rk)$ for every $r\in\mathbb{Z}$. Therefore every finite composition of these translations is a translation by
$$
\left(h\sum_i m_i c^{r_i},k\sum_i m_i c^{r_i}\right),
$$
where $m_i,r_i\in\mathbb{Z}$. Define $H_0=\left\{\sum_i m_i c^{r_i}:m_i,r_i\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}$ and $H=hH_0$. Since $c>0$ and $c\neq1$, either $c^n\to0$ as $n\to+\infty$ or $c^n\to0$ as $n\to-\infty$. Hence $H_0$ contains positive elements arbitrarily close to $0$. We now claim that $H_0$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$. Let $u\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $\delta\in H_0$ with $0<\delta<2\varepsilon$. Let $m\in\mathbb{Z}$ be an integer nearest to $\frac{u}{\delta}$. Then $|u-m\delta|\le\frac{\delta}{2}<\varepsilon$. Since $m\cdot\delta\in H_0$, every real number can be approximated arbitrarily closely by elements of $H_0$. Thus $H_0$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$, and since $h\neq0$, $H=hH_0$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$. Put $\lambda=\frac{k}{h}$. Every translation constructed above has the form $(t,\lambda t)$ with $t\in H$. Therefore, for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$ and $t\in H$, the point $(x+t,f(x)+\lambda t)$ belongs to $\Gamma$. Hence $f(x+t)=f(x)+\lambda t$. $\square$

**Lemma 15.** Under the hypotheses of Lemma $14$, there exists $\lambda>0$ such that $f(x)=\lambda x$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$.

*Proof.* Fix $x>0$. By Lemma $8$, $f(x)>0$. Since $H$ is dense, there exist sequences $t_n,s_n\in H$ such that $t_n>-x$, $s_n<-x$, and $t_n\to-x$, $s_n\to-x$. Since $x+t_n>0$, Lemma $8$ gives $f(x+t_n)>0$. By Lemma $14$, $f(x)+\lambda t_n>0$. Likewise, $x+s_n<0$, so $f(x+s_n)<0$, and hence $f(x)+\lambda s_n<0$. If $\lambda<0$, then $f(x)+\lambda s_n\to f(x)-\lambda x>0$, contradicting $f(x)+\lambda s_n<0$. If $\lambda=0$, then $f(x)+\lambda s_n=f(x)>0$, again a contradiction. Hence $\lambda>0$. Taking limits in $f(x)+\lambda t_n>0$ gives $f(x)-\lambda x\ge0$, while taking limits in $f(x)+\lambda s_n<0$ gives $f(x)-\lambda x\le0$. Hence $f(x)=\lambda x$ for every $x>0$. By Lemma $6$, if $x<0$ then $f(x)=-f(-x)=\lambda x$, while Lemma $1$ gives $f(0)=0$. Therefore $f(x)=\lambda x$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$. $\square$

**Lemma 16.** Under the hypotheses of Lemma $14$, $\lambda=1$.

*Proof.* By Lemma $15$, $f(x)=\lambda x$ for every $x\in\mathbb{R}$, with $\lambda>0$. Substituting into the original equation gives $\lambda(x^2+\lambda xy)=\lambda^2x^2+\lambda xy$ for every $x,y\in\mathbb{R}$. Setting $y=0$ gives $\lambda x^2=\lambda^2x^2$ for every $x$. Taking $x=1$ gives $\lambda=\lambda^2$. Since $\lambda>0$, we obtain $\lambda=1$. Hence $f=\operatorname{id}$. $\square$

Therefore, if $f\not\equiv0$, then $f$ is the identity function and we are done. $\blacksquare$
