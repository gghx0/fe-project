We claim $f(x)=x$ and $f(x)=0$ are the only solutions, which work.

Denote the assertion by $P(x,y)$. Note $P(0,0)$ gives $f(0)^2=0$, hence $f(0)=0$. Next $P(x,0)$ gives $f(xf(x))=f(x^2)$.

Note that if $f$ is injective, this immediately implies $f(x)=x$ for all $x$. Hence suppose that for some $a,b$ we have $f(a)=f(b)=c$. First $P(a,b)$ and $P(b,a)$ give $f(ac+bc)=f(a^2)+c^2=f(b^2)+c^2$, so $f(a^2)=f(b^2)$.

Next take $P(a,x-a)$ and $P(b,x-b)$ to get $f(xf(a))=f(a^2)+cf(x-a)$ and likewise for $b$, but this implies $cf(x-a)=cf(x-b)$. Hence $c$ is zero, or $f(x-a)=f(x-b)$, hence $f$ is periodic with period $a-b$.

Assume the latter is never true, so $f$ is injective except for its roots. Hence from $f(xf(x))=f(x^2)$ we get $f(x^2)=0$ or $f(x)=x$.

Assume for some nonzero $x$ that $f(x^2)\ne 0$, so $f(x)=x$. If no $y\ne 0$ goes to zero, then $f$ is injective. Hence pick $y\ne 0$ with $f(0)=0$. Then $P(x,y)$ gives $f(x^2+xy)=f(x^2)$. Since $xy\ne 0$, we have that $x^2$ is a root, contradiction. Hence $f(x^2)=0$, so all nonnegatives map to zero.

Suppose there are $x,y$ with $f(x),f(y)\ne 0$. Then $P(x,y)$ and $P(y,x)$ give $f(xf(x)+yf(x))=f(x)f(y)=f(xf(y)+yf(y))$. Since $f(x)f(y)\ne 0$, injectivity gives $xf(x)+yf(x)=xf(y)+yf(y)$. Since $x,y$ must both be negative, this implies $f(x)=f(y)$. Since they are nonzero again, we get $x=y$. Hence there exists at most one value of $x$ with $f(x)$ nonzero.

Suppose such $x$ exists. For this value of $x$, take $P(x,x)$ giving $f(2xf(x))=f(x)^2$. Since $f(2xf(x))$ is nonzero, we get $2xf(x)=x$, so $f(x)=\frac12$. However, plugging in gives $\frac12=\frac14$, contradiction. Hence $f$ is constantly zero.

Otherwise, $f$ is periodic. Let $p$ be a period of $f$ and take $P(x+p,y)$ and $P(x,y+p)$ to get $f(xf(x)+yf(x)+pf(x))=f((x+p)^2)+f(x)f(y)=f(x^2)+f(x)f(y)$. Hence $f(x^2)=f((x+p)^2)$, and similarly these equal $f((x+2p)^2)$. Assume $f(x^2)\ne 0$, so that from our injectivity argument it follows that $(x+p)^2-x^2=2xp+p^2$ is a period. Similarly $(x+2p)^2-(x+p)^2=2xp+3p^2$ is a period. Taking thrice the former minus the latter gives $4xp$ is a period.

Taking the period $p$ here to be $\frac14$ gives that $x$ is a period, but $f(x)=0$ implies $f(xf(x))=f(x^2)=0$, contradiction, so $\frac14$ is not a period. Taking the period $p$ here to itself be $4xp$ gives that $16x^2p$ is a period. Hence if $x$ is positive we have that $f(x)\ne 0$ implies $16xp$ is a period.

In fact we can remove the positivity constraint, since if $q$ is a period then we can pick a positive integer $n$ such that $x+nq$ is positive, then from $f(x+nq)=f(x)\ne 0$ we have $16p(x+nq)$ is a period. We also get $16p(x+(n+1)q)$ is a period, so their difference $16pq$ is a period, hence $16px$ is a period as required.

Let $y$ be a root of $f$ and suppose $x$ has $f(x^2)\ne 0$. Then $f(xf(x)+yf(x))=f(x^2)=f(xf(x))\ne 0$ gives $yf(x)$ is a period by our injectivity argument. Hence if $f$ is not constantly zero, by periodicity we can choose such an $x^2$, which implies $f(x)=c\ne 0$ from $f(xf(x))=f(x^2)$.

Hence we have that for each $x$, if it goes to zero, then $cx$ is a period, otherwise $16px$ is a period. Since $\frac14$ is not a period, taking $x=\frac1{64p}$ must give that $\frac c{64p}$ is a period. Hence taking $p$ to be this in $16px$, we get that for each $x$ at least one of $cx$ or $\frac{cx}{4p}$ is a period. Changing variables, we get that at least one of $x$ or $4px$ is a period.

Hence if $x$ is not a period then $4px$ is, and so is $16px$. However we have shown that if $q$ is a period, then $16pq$ is a period. Hence $16px$ is always a period. Since this takes every real value, we get that every real is a period of $f$, hence $f$ is constantly zero.

This resolves all cases, hence $f(x)=x$ and $f(x)=0$ are the only solutions.
