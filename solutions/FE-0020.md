We claim the only solutions are $f(x)\equiv x$ and $f(x)\equiv -x$, which obviously work. We now show they are the only solutions. Let $P(x,y)$ denote the assertion that $f(xf(x)+y)=x^2+f(y)$. 
***
Since $P(x,0)$ and $P(x,-xf(x))$ implies that $f(xf(x))=x^2+f(0)$ and $f(-xf(x))=f(0)-x^2$, $f$ is surjective.

Let $a$ be the real number such that $f(a)=0$. $P(a,y)$ implies that $a=0$. Hence $f(0)=0$ and $f(r)=0\implies r=0$ for all $r$. In particular, if for some $c$, we have $f(x)=f(x+c)$ for all real $x$, then $c=0$.

Let $b$ be the real number such that $f(b)=1$. $P(b,0)$ implies that $b^2=1$. Since it's easy to prove that $f(x)$ is a solution iff $g(x)\equiv f(-x)$ is a solution, we can assume WLOG that $b=1\implies f(1)=1$.

$P(x,0)\implies f(xf(x))=x^2$
$P(1,x)\implies f(x+1)=f(x)+1$
$P(x+1,y), P(x,f(x)+x+y+1)\implies f(f(x)+x+y+1)-f(y)=2x+1\implies f(f(x)+x+y)-f(y)=2x$.
From the above equation, we can see that
$$
\begin{align*}
f(f(x)+f(y)+x+y+z) &= f(f(x)+x+z)+2y\\
&= f(z)+2x+2y\\
&=f(f(x+y)+(x+y)+z)\\
\implies f(x)+f(y)&=f(x+y)
\end{align*}
$$

Hence, $f$ is additive. 
Thus, $P(f(x)+x,0)\implies 2f(xf(x))+2f(x^2)=(f(x)+x)^2\implies 2(x^2+f(x^2))=(f(x)+x)^2.$

Finally, we have 
$$
\begin{align*}
f(f(2x^2)+2x^2+y) &= 4x^2+f(y)\\
f(2(f(x^2)+x^2)+y)&= f(2xf(2x)+y)\\
f((f(x)+x)^2+y)&=f(4xf(x)+y)\\
\implies (f(x)+x)^2&=4xf(x)\\
\implies f(x)=x
\end{align*}
$$

Hence, the only solution (with the assumption that $b=1$) is the identity function. Generalize back, we get that the solutions are $\boxed{f(x)\equiv x}$ and $\boxed{f(x)\equiv -x.}$
